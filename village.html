Douma, Batroun district

Six hundred red roofs on a horseshoe of hillside.

Douma sits at 1,150 metres, arranged in a curve around its own valley so that every house looks at every other house. It has been a market town since the eighteenth century, it empties in February and fills in August, and it has never once been in a hurry.

What people actually do here

Not much, on purpose. These are the three things everyone ends up doing anyway.

Four minutes on foot

The old souk

One long street of stone shopfronts, built when Douma traded silk and never rebuilt since. About sixty are still open: a saddle-maker, two carpenters, a printer with a hand-set press, the sweet shop with the good knefeh. Quietest before ten, busiest on Sunday after church.

Two hours, easy, down and back

The olive terraces

The path starts at our lower gate and drops through eleven terraces to the riverbed, past walls that have been restacked by hand every spring for two centuries. Harvest is late October and early November, and if you are here then you will be handed a rake.

All afternoon, if you like

The stairs and the roofs

Douma is stitched together by public staircases — dozens of them, most older than the road. Climb any of them to the top of the village for the view that every photograph of Douma is taken from: the whole horseshoe of red roofs at once.

Worth the drive

  • Bchaaleh and its ancient olive grove — sixteen trees said to be six thousand years old. 15 minutes.
  • The Baatara gorge waterfall, falling three hundred metres through a limestone bridge. 35 minutes, best in April and May.
  • The Tannourine cedar reserve, quieter and larger than the Chouf. 45 minutes.
  • Batroun for the sea, the lemonade and a swim. 45 minutes down, and worth doing in the late afternoon.

Getting here

Eighty-five kilometres from Beirut. Allow an hour and three quarters, or two hours on a Friday evening. The road is paved the whole way and perfectly fine in a normal car from April to November.

  1. Leave Beirut on the coastal highway heading north, past Jounieh, Byblos and Amchit. About 60 km, an hour without traffic.

  2. Exit at Batroun and take the inland road signposted Kfar Hilda and Douma. The climb starts here.

  3. Twenty-five kilometres of switchbacks through Kfar Hilda and Bchaaleh, climbing to 1,150 m. Forty-five minutes. Sound your horn on the blind corners; everybody does.

  4. In Douma, follow the souk to its upper end. Our gate is the one with the two cypresses, fifty metres past the church. Park inside it.

Without a car: a service taxi from Dora in Beirut to Batroun costs about $6, and a taxi up from Batroun about $25. Tell us your arrival time and we can send someone down for $30, or meet you at the airport for $70.

Season by season

We are open all twelve months. Each one is a different village.

March to May

12–22 °C · the waterfall is at full flow

Everything green at once, wild cyclamen in the terraces, and the Baatara gorge at its loudest. The best walking of the year, and the quietest house.

June to August

18–29 °C · cool by nine in the evening

The village doubles in size, the souk stays open late, and there is a festival in August with a stage in the square. Book well ahead; weekends go first.

September to November

12–25 °C · olive harvest from late October

Our favourite. Warm days, cold mornings, the presses running at night, and the whole valley smelling of crushed olives.

December to February

2–11 °C · snow two or three times

Wood stove lit from four in the afternoon, breakfast moved into the vaulted room, and almost nobody about. Bring proper shoes and check with us before driving up after a storm.
